2 · The problem: one morning in Chennai

0:40 – 1:50

I'd like you to imagine a morning after heavy rain in Chennai.

By eight o'clock, the Collector's office is already flooded too, with information. Citizens have filed complaints about waterlogged streets. The police have logged calls about a fallen tree. PWD has opened work orders for blocked drains. IMD has issued a warning. And the newspapers, in English and in Tamil, are already reporting all of it.

All of this is useful. But it arrives from a dozen different places, in different formats, at different times, and none of it talks to each other.

So the same waterlogged street can show up as seven separate reports. Nobody can see that it's one problem. And nobody can answer the two questions that actually matter to a Collector: What is really happening in my district? And what needs me first?

Today, that answer depends on phone calls, meetings and a lot of experience. We believe it should take one screen.

3 · Our vision

1:50 – 2:20

So our vision for District IQ was simple. Not another dashboard that shows more data, but a system that shows less, but the right things, and always explains why.

It should collect on its own, connect the dots on its own, and give the Collector a clear, trustworthy picture every single morning.

4 · What makes it different

2:20 – 3:50

There are five things we're especially proud of, and you'll see every one of them today.

First, it never sleeps. Every hour, it collects fresh data on its own: weather and warnings, flood levels, air quality, lake levels, market prices and news. And it does it responsibly. It never breaks into a website or bypasses a captcha.

Second, it connects the dots. Seven reports about one street become one incident, even when one report is in Tamil and another is in English. Matching a Tamil report with an English one, when they share almost no words, was one of the hardest things we built.

Third, it explains itself. Every incident has a priority, and the reasons are written right next to it. Nothing is a black box. And over time, it learns from the Collector's own decisions.

Fourth, you can trust its numbers. We use AI where it genuinely helps: to read, match and write. But every number is checked against the real data before anyone sees it. Even our chatbot is built so that it cannot invent a figure.

And fifth, it closes the loop. The Collector can send work to a department, the officer has to show photo proof that it's done, and the citizen who complained can see that proof too. One system, from the citizen's complaint to the Collector's decision, and back.

5 · Who it serves

3:50 – 4:15

It serves three kinds of people. The Collector, who sees the whole district. Department officers, 25 departments, each with a clear queue of their own work. And citizens, who can file a complaint in a few taps and follow it until it's done. All of it works in Tamil as well as English.

Thank you, [friend's name].

So you've just seen what the Collector sees every morning. Now I'd like to take you behind the screen: how it knows all this, how it joins thousands of scattered reports into one clear picture, and how it reaches the officers and the citizens. Let's start where everything starts: the data.

Click the "Collected …" chip in the top bar. Data sources opens.

Everything starts here. This little chip tells the Collector when the data was last collected and whether every source is up to date. Right now, all eight are current, and this refreshes every hour, on its own.

These are our sources: IMD weather and its district warnings, the Chennai Flood Monitor's river levels, CPCB air quality, Chennai Metro Water's lake levels, market prices, and news from six Chennai newspapers in English and Tamil.

Now, the first real problem we hit: government data is not built to be collected. CPCB has no open feed at all. And two of these sites simply block cloud servers, so our hourly job kept failing.

The challenge was that they didn't agree with each other: each one had its own idea of when it rained. So we gave them one shared world, with one rain calendar and Chennai's real wards. Now when it rains, complaints, police calls and drain work all rise together, just like real life. Every simulated record is marked, and plugging in a real department later is one small connector.

2 · One incident, from seven reports

2:30 – 5:30 · short
Open the severe incident you chose before the meeting (one with several sources and news).

Let me open one incident. This is the part of the project we're proudest of, and honestly the part that took the longest.

In the real world, one problem doesn't arrive as one report. A waterlogged street comes in as three citizen complaints, a police report, a PWD work order, and two news articles, one in Tamil and one in English. To a normal system, that's seven separate things. Here, it's one incident. One timeline, and the evidence from every source right here.

Getting this right was hard, and I'll tell you about three problems we had to solve.

The first: Tamil and English. A Tamil article and an English article about the same accident share almost no words. Our first approach simply couldn't tell "the same accident" apart from "two different accidents on the same day". So we rebuilt it to compare meaning, not words, using a multilingual AI model that understands both languages. A second model then reads the two articles side by side, the way a person would. Today, about 2,800 Tamil and English reports are matched this way.

The second: chains. If report A looks like B, and B looks a bit like C, a naive system pulls all three together, even when A and C are completely unrelated. We saw that happen, so we stopped it. Every match has to stand on its own.

The third, and this one taught us the most: coincidences. When we checked real news against our department records by hand, 141 pairs of them, we found the system sometimes joined two events just because they happened at the same place on the same day. A road-rage report and a murder story, for example. So we added a simple rule: they must also be the same kind of incident. Wrong joins dropped from 32 to 9. That's how we've worked the whole way through: test on real data, find what's wrong, fix it.

The result: when this system says two reports are the same incident, it's right 97 times out of 100. And when it isn't sure, it doesn't guess. It puts the pair on a review list for a person.

Duplicates: blocking + SimHash + union-find. Linking: KD-tree + logistic regression on 9 features + multilingual-e5 embeddings.
Tamil–English stories: bge-m3 embeddings + bge-reranker-v2-m3 cross-encoder + trained classifier + LLM check.

3 · Why this one is at the top

5:30 – 7:00 · short
Point at the severity, the priority and the list of reasons in the pop-up.

Now, the second question: why does this need the Collector first?

Most systems give you a score and expect you to trust it. We give you the reasons. You can read them right here: severe, past its deadline, not yet checked on the ground, vulnerable people nearby, linked to rain. Every point is explained. Nothing is a black box.

We had to think carefully about one trap here. One busy street can collect thirty complaints about the same pothole, while a single report of a live electric wire is far more dangerous. If every complaint simply added points, the pothole would win. That's the wrong order. So complaints count with diminishing weight: more complaints still raise priority, but they can never outweigh real danger.

And here's what makes it smarter over time: it learns from the Collector. Every time the Collector verifies, reopens or escalates something, that decision is recorded. After thirty decisions, the system adjusts its own weighting to match the Collector's judgement. And it only switches to the learned version if that version actually ranks better. So it adapts, but it can't drift off course.

Severity: rule-based score 0–100. Priority: weighted signals, log scale for sources and complaints.
Learning: logistic regression with a Gaussian (Bayesian) prior, adopted only if held-out ranking (AUC) improves.

8 · Warnings people can trust

13:30 – 14:45
Trends page → Unusual rises. Then Recurring hotspots.

Here's a challenge every alert system faces. If it cries wolf too often, people stop reading it. When we started, a simple "is today higher than usual?" rule gave us more than twenty alerts a week, and most of them were noise.

The big reason was rain. Of course waterlogging complaints jump when it rains. That's not news. So now every problem type, in every zone, learns its own normal level, including how much it normally rises on rain days and on particular weekdays. On top of that, we run a statistical correction, because when you check four thousand things a day, some will look unusual just by chance.

Today, the old rule would have raised fifteen alerts. Ours raised one. So when this page says something is rising, it's worth the Collector's time.

And here, recurring hotspots: places where the same problem keeps coming back. Today we found about 330. These are the places where one permanent fix, a new drain or a new streetlight, saves a hundred future complaints.

Optional, 15 seconds: click Environment & markets.

And for completeness: rainfall, air quality, reservoirs, lake levels and vegetable prices, all live, on one page.

Poisson baselines with weekday and rain factors; spike test + CUSUM; Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery control at 5%.
Hotspots: DBSCAN (haversine distance, 5 incidents within 250 m) + Getis-Ord Gi* on wards.

9 · Just ask

14:45 – 16:30 · short
Click Ask. Type "theft cases by zone this month". Then "show as a chart". Pin it. Then ask one question in Tamil.

Now, not every Collector wants to click through pages. Sometimes you just want to ask. So we built Ask District IQ.

[Type the question.] There's the answer, by zone. [Ask for a chart.] Now as a chart. [Pin it.] And it's pinned to the dashboard.

Here's the challenge we had to solve. AI chatbots are great with words, but they make up numbers. In government, a made-up number is not acceptable. Simulated departments

Grievances (about 16,100), police (about 12,300), PWD (about 2,100) and hospital alert episodes, 180 days. One shared rain calendar (dintel/sync.py) so all sources react to the same rain days. Every row has is_synthetic = 1.

The pipeline, step by step

  1. Load. One loader per source turns every record into a row of one events table (70 columns: time, text, category, place, impact, severity, status, provenance). Mobile numbers become a keyed hash, so personal data is never copied.
  2. Classify. Structured sources use crosswalk tables to our ~31 categories. Free text uses a character n-gram classifier (scikit-learn), which handles English, Tamil and Tanglish. News is labelled by an LLM (Groq), and a local SetFit model (multilingual-e5-base) trained on those labels covers articles the LLM hasn't reached. A trained incident filter decides which articles are real incidents and which are notices, politics or court news.
  3. Locate. Point-in-polygon on the 200 GCC ward boundaries; a point within 500 m outside a ward is snapped (and flagged). Ward → zone, and ward → taluk by majority vote. News places come from the gazetteer, which picks where it happened, not every place mentioned.
  4. Shared world (overlay). The simulators were written separately, so they disagreed about rain days. The overlay builds one world rain calendar and adds the reports a source would have produced on those days. It also plants multi-source events, which give us an answer key to measure the linker. Real sources are never changed.
  5. Duplicates within a source. Compare only plausible pairs (blocking), apply the portal's rule for grievances plus officers' own "duplicate of" decisions, then union-find joins them into groups. Measured with B-cubed F1 ≈ 0.995.
  6. News stories across languages. bge-m3 embeddings put Tamil and English in one vector space to find candidate pairs; the bge-reranker-v2-m3 cross-encoder reads each pair together; a small model trained on our labelled pairs decides; an LLM checks unsure pairs; and we stop chaining (A like B, B like C does not pull in C). About 9,500 story groups became 7,100; about 2,800 merges joined Tamil and English reports.
  7. Link across sources into incidents. Blocking by category group, a KD-tree on distance and a time window per category → a logistic regression scores each pair on 9 features: distance, time gap, word similarity, meaning similarity (multilingual-e5), shared named things, same category, same ward, coarse location, involves news → thresholds learned from the data (merge at 0.80, review band from 0.55) → union-find. A news report links to a department record only if both have the same category. F1 ≈ 0.83 on the held-out half (precision 0.97, recall 0.72).
  8. Incidents: severity, deadline, priority. Lead record, status and a deadline per category (an SLA). Severity is a rule score from 0 to 100 (≥70 Severe, ≥50 High, ≥30 Medium, else Low), from facts only. Priority = severity + weighted signals, each with its reason (table below). Priority is computed when it is read, so it never goes stale.
  9. Analytics. Poisson baselines with weekday and rain factors; one-day spikes plus CUSUM slow rises; all ~3,900 tests under one Benjamini-Hochberg FDR at 5%. DBSCAN hotspots, Getis-Ord Gi* on wards, EWMA and z-scores for trends, KPIs per period and zone, and PSI for category drift.
  10. Agents. Data Steward (feed health, data contract checks, quarantine, drift), Action Planner (playbook tasks per category), Gap Finder (in the news, no department record), Linker (pairs in the review band), Watchdog (alerts), Briefing (writes the daily, weekly, monthly and quarterly briefings; a numeric verifier rejects any number not in the data: 0 unverified today).
  11. Store and publish. SQLite store (33 tables) → export_intel.py uploads each changed table to S3 as gzip JSON, named by its hash, and then a manifest. Unchanged tables are skipped.
  12. Website reads it. On start and every few minutes, the site downloads the manifest and tables into an in-memory SQLite database (Node's built-in node:sqlite). Its queries were written for MySQL, so a small dialect layer translates them. Build hooks add headlines, incident clusters and lake alerts. Writes (complaints, decisions) go to DynamoDB through API Gateway and a Lambda.

Ask District IQ, step by step

  1. Code guard. Rejects unsafe or off-topic input; fixes spelling; detects Tamil.
  2. Router (a fast model, or rules if no AI is available) decides the kind of question: a count, a list, a chart, an explanation, a console action, or a chart edit.
  3. Tools or query plan. Either one of the console's own functions, or a planner writes a structured plan that a compiler turns into read-only SQL. The model never writes raw SQL.
  4. Search for open questions: LanceDB (embedded vector database) with multilingual-e5-base vectors, plus BM25 keyword search, merged by reciprocal-rank fusion. It retrieves records; counts still come from SQL.
  5. Facts → composer. Code builds the facts; the composer model writes the answer in English or Tamil.
  6. Number verifier. Every number in the text must exist in the facts. If not, it regenerates once, then falls back to a template. A chart checker validates the chart type.
  7. At most three model calls per question. If the AI is busy or missing, the answer still comes from the tools and says "without AI". Officers' answers are locked to their department.

Likely questions

Short, honest answers. If you don't know, say "we haven't measured that yet; here is what we did measure."

Is the data real?

News, IMD weather and warnings, CPCB air quality, the Flood Monitor, lake levels and market prices are real and collected every hour. Grievances, police, PWD and hospital records are simulated, because those departments have no open API. They share the real rain calendar and real wards, and every simulated row is flagged. Connecting a real department means writing one loader.

Why did you not use the two FarmwiseAI systems?

We explored both. The hostels system is for GHMC, which is Hyderabad, and the leave portal is for Yadadri Bhuvanagiri in Telangana. Neither has Chennai data. The same kind of source, a website with a login, plugs in through Data Studio.

How do you know two reports are the same incident?

We compare only plausible pairs: same category group, nearby, and close in time. A logistic regression scores each pair on distance, time gap, word and meaning similarity, shared names, category and ward. Pairs above a threshold learned from the data are merged; the grey zone goes to a review list. News must also share the category. F1 is about 0.83 on held-out simulated events.

How do you match Tamil and English news?

They rarely share words, so we match by meaning. bge-m3 puts both languages in one vector space; a cross-encoder reranks the candidates; a model trained on our labelled pairs decides; an LLM checks only the unsure pairs. We also prevent chaining.

How is priority decided? Is it AI?

No. Severity is a rule score from facts. Priority adds weighted, named signals: past deadline, more than one source, vulnerable people, rain, growing, recurring. Each reason is shown. The weights can learn from the Collector's decisions after 30 decisions, and only if they rank better on held-out decisions.

How do you avoid false alerts?

Each category-zone series has a Poisson baseline adjusted for weekday and rain. We run about 4,000 tests a day and apply one Benjamini-Hochberg correction at a 5% false-discovery rate. Today that kept 1 alert where a simple rule would have raised 15.

Can the chatbot make up numbers?

No. Counts come from read-only SQL that our compiler generates from a structured plan. The model only plans and writes words. Every number in the text is checked against the data; if one fails, it rewrites once, then uses a template. 75 out of 75 golden questions are correct with AI off.

Which AI models do you use, and why?

Amazon Bedrock Nova Lite for routing and writing (it is on the FarmwiseAI AWS account), with Gemini and Groq as backups. Local multilingual-e5 and bge models for meaning. Numbers never come from a model, which keeps it cheap, testable and safe.

What is your weakest point?

The news incident filter: F1 about 0.63, so it finds about 6 in 10 incident articles. More real labels are the fastest fix, and that is first on our roadmap. Also, linking accuracy is measured on simulated events because the department data is simulated.

What happens if a source goes down?

The Data Steward marks the feed as stale or degraded, the chip in the top bar turns orange and says which feed is behind, and the rest of the dashboard keeps working on the last good data. Endpoints closed by their owner (captcha, login) are logged, never bypassed.

Why GitHub Actions and not AWS for scheduling?

Our AWS role does not allow EventBridge scheduling, so cron-job.org triggers the GitHub job every hour. The job runs the pipeline and uploads to S3. CPCB and the Flood Monitor block cloud machines, so the team PC collects those two and sends them to the job.

Why is the website's database SQLite in memory?

We were asked to use only AWS services, with no SQL server. Each hourly build sits in S3; the site loads it into an in-memory SQLite database, which is fast and needs no server. Durable data (accounts, complaints, decisions) is written to DynamoDB through API Gateway and a Lambda.

How is personal data protected?

Mobile numbers are hashed in the pipeline, ID numbers are encrypted, passwords are bcrypt-hashed, roles are enforced on the server, real complaints are excluded from the public build files, and every Collector decision is audited.

How does adding a new source by link work?

Paste a link in Data Studio. The agent checks the host is public and robots.txt allows it, detects RSS, API, page or login, maps the fields with AI, and shows a preview. Nothing is saved until the Collector approves. Scheduled runs then use the saved mapping without AI.

How does the officer–Collector loop work?

Severe incident → the Collector approves an AI-drafted instruction to the department officer → the officer does the work and sends a completion report with photos → it appears in the Collector's My Tasks → the Collector verifies or sends it back. Every step is logged.

How do you place a news article on the map?

A Tamil-aware gazetteer of Chennai places finds mentions. A place-role step picks where the event happened, not, for example, the hospital a victim was taken to. Then point-in-polygon finds the ward. Articles that only say "Chennai" are not pinned.

How did you test the system?

About 570 automated web tests, about 100 pipeline tests and 88 news tests, a golden set of 75 chatbot questions checked by SQL, and an evaluation report each build with the accuracy of every AI step.

How would this scale to other districts?

Collectors and categories are configuration; ward boundaries, taluks and departments are reference tables. A new district needs its boundaries, its gazetteer and its department list. The pipeline and the website stay the same.

What would you do with more time?

Connect one real department system, label more real news to raise the filter's recall, collect 30 Collector decisions so priority learns, and move the PC collectors to an approved server.
